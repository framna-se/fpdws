Workshop 3

Constraint cards

Every table gets the same question. Each table draws a card that rules out one way of answering it, and plans the cheapest way that's left.

45 minutes

The question

You're on the Parkinator 2000 team. This email arrived this morning.

From
Head of Product
To
The app team
Subject
Parkinator Plus

We're planning a paid tier for Q1: Parkinator Plus, 29 kr a month. No ads, a monthly receipt sent straight to your employer, and extending a parking session by text message.

Before we spend a quarter building it, I'd like to know whether drivers will actually pay for it, not just say they would. Can you find out?

Will drivers actually pay 29 kr a month for Parkinator Plus, or do they just say they would?

Every table has this question. What differs is the card you draw.

What you have to work with

The app

  • 120,000 drivers use it each month, about 35,000 parking sessions a day, in Stockholm, Gothenburg and Malmö
  • A release goes out every Thursday. Feature flags and staged rollouts (for example to 5 percent of drivers) are in place.
  • Remote config can change prices and text, and show or hide any existing screen or button, with no release
  • A hidden "Upgrade" row in Settings and a "Coming soon" screen are still in the app from an old project. Remote config can switch them on.
  • Ads on the parking session screen bring in about 40,000 kr a month

What's already known

  • Every screen view and tap is logged and searchable. Nobody has looked at the data for months.
  • The support inbox gets about 300 emails a week

People

  • The team: you, four other engineers and one designer. There's no product manager right now.
  • Tomas, Nadia and Jonas from this morning are three of your drivers. Use what they told you.

Your constraint

Each card rules out one way of answering the question. Your job is to find another.

One person at the table presses Draw and reads the card out loud. That card is your table's constraint. If two people pressed, the first card counts.

The rule.

Still allowed.

This card stays on this phone. Read it out so the whole table has it.

What to do at your table

You have about 25 minutes. We'll call time. You're not designing Plus. You're planning how to find out whether drivers would pay for it.

  1. One person presses Draw and reads the card out loud.
  2. 90 seconds on your own, in your notebook. Write down ways to find out whether drivers would pay that your card still allows. One line per idea.
  3. Pick a scribe. Go round the table, one idea each, until nobody has any left.
  4. Agree on one plan: the cheapest thing that would give you a real answer. The scribe writes down three things:
    • what you'd do
    • when you'd have an answer (a day or a week)
    • what result would make you say no to Plus
  5. When we call "five minutes left", the scribe puts your plan into Menti (code on the screen). One answer per table: The card / What you would do. Keep the other two in the notebook; we may ask.
  6. Finished before that? Open "Finished?" below.
Stuck? Open this

Work backwards. What result would make you say no to Plus? What's the smallest thing that could produce that result under your card?

Your card removes one route. Look at what's left: the app you can change without a release, the data that's already logged, the three drivers from this morning, the things you could fake. The list from the last talk is a good checklist: a paper sketch, a clickable mock, a button that leads to "coming soon", a person doing by hand what the software would do, a real build behind a flag. Which is the cheapest one your card allows?
